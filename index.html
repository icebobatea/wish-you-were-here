Wish You Were Here

A century before the selfie, you proved you'd been somewhere by posting a picture of it. Museums Victoria holds nearly a thousand of those pictures. Read together, they trace who Victorians were, where they came from, and who they wrote home to.

Where the postcards take us

Pick a card at random from the museum's drawers and it will most likely show one building: Melbourne's Royal Exhibition Building. It was built for the 1880 International Exhibition, and in 1901 Australia's first Parliament opened under its dome. Souvenir printers never tired of it.

The second cluster is stranger. Egypt and Iraq are almost all soldiers' cards. The Peile brothers filled an album with views of Heliopolis while camped near Cairo in 1914–16, and another soldier kept a set of monitor ships on the Tigris.

The golden age of the postcard

Seven in ten dated cards come from just twenty years, 1900–1919, when cards were cheap to print and cheap to post. But the spikes inside that boom have two very different causes.

Real people writing. From 1904, Marion and Janet Flinn in Melbourne sent card after card to the Scott family in Paisley: Collins Street, the Town Hall, the Black Spur. Their 52 cards make up most of the museum's correspondence cards.

Curators guessing. An undated card gets a best estimate such as "circa 1905" or "1916–1919", and the guesses pile onto one year. 77 of the 96 cards dated 1905 are estimates, so treat the tallest bars as rough.

Spread over decades, each theme has its own moment:

  1. A The Flinns' cards to Paisley, 1904–07.
  2. B 306 soldiers' cards, 184 of them from the RSL's collection.
  3. C Cards bought on the voyage out after the war, in Algiers and Ceylon in 1951.
  4. D Protest cards such as Avant Card's "Refugee Week" (2004). Migration is still on postcards, now as politics.

What they showed

Shrink the whole collection to 100 cards and 35 come from soldiers. 19 show the Exhibition Building, 15 belong to migrants, and only 8 are ordinary letters between friends.

Themes follow the museum's own catalogue, so they tell us why a card was kept as much as what it shows.

Then 1920 flips the collection over. War cards fall from almost half to one in twenty: the soldiers came home, and with them went the reason to buy cards in Cairo.

Migration rises by 32 points. Across the middle of the century, and especially after the 1947 migration scheme, ships brought hundreds of thousands of new Victorians. The cards they bought on the way are what the museum now holds.

A warning before trusting any of this. A museum holds what people give it. For four in ten cards we don't know who donated them, and a few big gifts dominate: the RSL's war cards and the Exhibition Building's own archive.

So this is a portrait of what Victorians chose to keep, not of every card ever posted.

Postcards from the front

Each theme has a heartbeat. War cards stretch from the 1870s to the 1940s, yet half of them fall inside 1914–1916. Exhibition souvenirs crowd the decade after Parliament sat in the building. Migration cards beat slowly from the 1920s to the late 1950s, the age of the migrant ship.

Away from home, the places on the cards trace where soldiers served. Two collections fill most of this chart: one family's album from the camps outside Cairo, and one set from the Mesopotamian campaign along the Tigris.

Britain is different. It turns up under almost every theme, because it was where so many Victorians' families still lived.

Sent home

When a card records who sent it and to whom, the arrows almost all point one way: back to Britain. Fifty-two cards went from Australia to Scotland, most of them written by Marion and Janet Flinn to the Scotts in Paisley, picture cards of Melbourne and the Victorian bush written between 1904 and 1907.

Why Scotland? In 1911 one Victorian in seven had been born overseas, and most of those in Britain or Ireland. For them a penny postcard was the cheapest way to show the family what the new life looked like.

The surprise is China. Over 4,000 Chinese-born Victorians were still here in 1911, the last of the gold-rush miners, their numbers shrinking under the 1901 Immigration Restriction Act.

The same waves run through the postcards. British and Irish birthplaces fade as the gold-rush generation dies out. After 1947, Italy and Greece surge, the same years migrant-voyage cards fill the collection.

After the White Australia policy ended in 1973, Asia slowly takes over. By 2011 more Victorians were born in India than in Italy. The museum's newest migration cards are about refugees, not voyages.

Close to home

Inside Victoria, the cards pile up on central Melbourne and its inner suburbs, mostly souvenirs of the city itself.

The big exception sits in the Gippsland ranges: Walhalla, a gold-mining town that once held thousands. Its railway arrived only in 1910, a few years before the last big mine closed. The 36 cards in the Rodda Collection catch a town at its final peak, before it emptied.
