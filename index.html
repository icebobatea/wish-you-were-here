Melbourne · 1880–2015

Wish You Were Here

Before phones had cameras, a postcard was how you showed someone where you were. Museums Victoria keeps nearly a thousand of them — souvenirs of a grand exhibition, notes from soldiers in the desert, and messages from migrants to the families they left behind. Here is what they tell us.

Where the postcards take us

Most of the cards picture Australia, and most of those picture one building: Melbourne's Royal Exhibition Building. But a second cluster sits half a world away, in Egypt and Iraq. These are the postcards Australian soldiers bought and sent home during the First World War.

The golden age of the postcard

About seven in ten dated cards in the collection come from just twenty years. Between 1900 and 1919, cheap printing and a reliable postal service made the postcard the text message of its day.

The two spikes have different causes. The first, around 1904–1906, is mostly souvenir cards of the Exhibition Building and everyday notes between friends. The second, 1914–1916, is almost entirely the war.

What they showed

If the whole collection were shrunk to 100 postcards, a third would be from soldiers and about one in five would show the Exhibition Building.

Themes come from how the museum itself catalogued each card.

After 1920 the mix changes completely. War cards almost disappear, and migration takes their place: shipping-line cards, souvenirs of the voyage out, and later, protest cards about refugees.

A caution before reading too much into this. A museum keeps what people donate. Over a third of the cards belong to no named collection, and most of the rest came in a few large donations from the RSL and the Exhibition Building's own archive.

Postcards from the front

Soldiers' cards span from the 1870s to the 1940s, but half of them were made in just three years, 1914 to 1916. Exhibition souvenirs cluster just as tightly in the decade after Federation, while migration cards spread across the whole twentieth century.

Away from home, almost every place on a card is somewhere Australian troops served: the camps around Cairo, the Mesopotamian campaign in Iraq, the hospitals of England, and the Western Front.

Sent home

Some cards record who sent them and to whom. Most international ones go one way, from Australia back to Britain. Fifty-two went to Scotland, most of them written by Marion and Janet Flinn in Melbourne to J. B. Scott in Paisley: picture cards of a new country, sent home between 1904 and 1907.

Why Scotland? In 1911, one Victorian in seven had been born overseas, and most of them in Britain or Ireland. For them, a postcard was the cheapest way to show the family back home what their new life looked like.

That pattern did not last. British and Irish birthplaces dominated until the Second World War. Then came a wave from Italy and Greece, the same years migration cards take over the collection. By 2011, India and China had overtaken them both.

Close to home

Inside Victoria the cards cluster around central Melbourne and its inner suburbs. The one big exception is Walhalla, a gold-mining town in the Gippsland ranges, captured in a set of 36 cards in the museum's Rodda Collection.
